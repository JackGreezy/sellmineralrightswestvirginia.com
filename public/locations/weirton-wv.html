Sell Mineral Rights in Weirton, WV

Most of what sits under Weirton's older residential and river-bottom tracts was leased before anyone here had heard the word Marcellus, and that lease language still controls what an owner is owed today.

Weirton grew up around steel, not gas, and the mineral estate under most of the city's older lots was severed decades ago as a routine part of farm and town-lot conveyances long before any operator had drilled a lateral in Brooke County. That severance history is exactly what we start with: pulling the Brooke County deed record to see who actually holds the mineral interest under a given Weirton parcel, whether it was ever leased, and on what terms.

A fair number of those old severances carry flat-rate gas leases from the shallow-well era, the kind that pay a fixed annual sum regardless of what the well produces. If a modern operator ever drills near a Weirton tract still bound by one of those leases, West Virginia's statutory 1/8 royalty minimum requires the lease to convert before production royalty is owed on new wells covered by that statute — and plenty of owners have no idea their lease even qualifies.

Brooke County's Older Lease Stock

Brooke County was never a Marcellus hotspot the way Doddridge or Wetzel became, but it has a long production history in the shallow Gordon Sand and Big Injun horizons, going back to gas wells drilled well before the county had zoning or paved county roads outside the river towns. Many of those original leases were flat-rate instruments signed by landowners who wanted a small steady payment more than a share of production nobody expected to amount to much.

Those leases rarely got renegotiated. They got inherited. A well that has quietly paid the same fifty or one hundred dollars a year since the 1960s is still sitting on some Weirton-area deed, and the current owner of record is often three or four generations removed from whoever signed it.

What a Weirton Deed Record Actually Shows

The mineral interest under a Weirton lot is recorded separately from the surface, and the chain runs back through the Brooke County Clerk's office in Wellsburg. Pulling that chain tells us whether the interest passed cleanly through a will, sat in an unprobated estate, or splintered across siblings and cousins who never formally divided it.

It also tells us whether a lease is still active, has lapsed for lack of production, or was never properly released after an old well was plugged. Any of those change what an offer on the interest should look like.

Flat-Rate Leases Still on the Books

Converting a flat-rate lease to a royalty-paying one is a statutory process, not a negotiation you have to win on your own. If your Weirton tract is under one of these older leases and there is any current or planned drilling nearby, that conversion should happen before you sign anything else — it directly affects what an interest is worth and what we're able to offer for it.

We flag this on every flat-rate tract we look at in Brooke County before we quote a number, because valuing an interest against the wrong royalty structure produces a wrong number.

Heirship Behind Weirton Tracts

It's common to find a Weirton mineral interest held in undivided fractions by six, eight, or more descendants of the original owner, several of whom moved out of the Ohio Valley generations ago and may not know the interest exists. We've bought fractional interests as small as a sixty-fourth from heirs who were simply glad to convert a confusing paper asset into a check.

If your name is on a deed but you're not sure how large your share is, or whether an estate was ever formally settled, that's normal here and it's the first thing we sort out.

you only own a small fraction of the mineral rights under a Weirton tract. Can you still buy it?
